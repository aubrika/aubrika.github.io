## beginnings

When I first started martial arts at 17 at a little aikido dojo, I was not very coordinated. Having never done sports in any significant way, my body had some catching up to do and it felt like a long process. By the time I got to college and started training at the college judo club, I was a little more coordinated, but I still felt completely clueless about how to throw or pin anyone. I failed a lot. All I knew is that I had seen old black and white videos of Kyuzo Mifune doing beautiful judo, weighing even less than I did at the time, and I wanted to do that too.

When I was a sophomore I added Brazilian Jiujitsu to my training rotation and I was hooked for life. It gave my judo a larger game to live in, and I split my time between both sports for years. I approached judo like a kickboxer studying boxing: on the judo mat I was trying to sharpen specific tools for use in the bigger game of jiujitsu which had captured my imagination.

In those early days I still often had no idea *why* I succeeded or failed out on the mat, but I *was* learning. In my mind I started building models of what I was doing, and asking myself how best to understand such an expansive sport. My coach likens this initial state of trackless bewilderment to *snow blindness,* where a person is temporarily blinded by reflected light after walking out onto snow-covered terrain. Only in time and gradually do your eyes adjust and a landscape resolves around you.

## the ladder

Early in my training, someone said to me: jiujitsu is like a ladder. You can climb a rung to a better position, or go down a rung to a worse position. It is important, therefore, to understand which positions are better or worse, and how to “climb” the ladder. In the most basic sense you have to orient yourself towards a better position: pinning someone, rather than being pinned, for example.

![Five positions labeled A through E arranged as a vertical ladder, with arrows in both directions between neighboring rungs.](/images/jiujitsu-is-a-manifold/ladder.png)

The problem with this, I eventually realized, is that the rungs were not arrayed in a sequence: you could and did travel from one rung to several other rungs that seemed to be nearby. So I thought: jiujitsu must be something like a tree.

## a garden of many paths

When I had just started aikido, I thought that a specific attack (a karate chop to the side of your head, for example) had some single best response—or, at least, might have one. I didn't know what the best thing to do there was (I got karate-chopped in the head plenty of times), but clearly there were several options, since my sensei showed us tons of different blocks, throws, dodges, all kinds of things. I figured in time I would know which one to pick out of the big lookup table containing all the aikido moves: if they do this, I would do this. Easy. I just had to figure out why to do one thing over another, after I figured out how to make the thing work in the first place.

![A branching decision tree, with position A at the root and arrows leading through B and C to successively more positions.](/images/jiujitsu-is-a-manifold/tree.png)

I carried this concept with me into judo and jiujitsu and I began to slowly figure a few things out: if they are moving forwards, you throw them forwards, but if they are moving backwards—well, you throw them backwards. You choose the branch of travel that makes the most sense, costs the least effort, and has the greatest effect. The problem, of course, is that sometimes your opponents also move sideways, or up, or down, or roll, or stand up, or sit down—and they can do this from a number of starting positions. So I thought: jiujitsu must be something like a graph.

## a subway map of many stations

Eventually in math I found an analogy I tried to hold over jiujitsu like a lens: the *directed graph*. A directed graph is a kind of network of nodes (also called vertices), connected by edges (also called arcs) representing the paths or connections between them. I thought the various named positions of jiujitsu—like back mount, side control, or half guard—must be like the nodes, and things like sweeps, transitions, turnovers, or takedowns must be like the edges you could travel between them.

![A directed network of positions A through O with many connecting paths, including a highlighted cycle from A to B to C and back to A.](/images/jiujitsu-is-a-manifold/directed-graph.png)

I started building a kind of transit map in my head of all the different places you could go or be, and all the different things you could do to move between them. There seemed to be dozens, maybe hundreds, of positions—I wasn’t sure how to count them, how to divide up what often seemed like a continuous flow into meaningful discrete pieces. There were definite patterns, but also important differences between apparently similar positions. Many defied classification.

To make matters worse, there seemed to be a near-infinite number of ways to travel between named positions, and many of those paths concealed important intermediate arrangements, while the “stations” I had labeled contained their own detailed internal geography. My subway map started feeling coarse and patchy, and I could never quite draw it in two dimensions to my satisfaction. I saw that every notable jiujitsu position had two inhabitants with very different reviews of the position in question: where one was having a great time and the other really wasn't.

Even if I couldn't number the stations, or the routes, or tell you where the center is, it was still true that some positions were “closer” to others in kind of adjacent way, and it was likewise somehow true that every *peak* of advantage on the landscape was also a *valley* of disadvantage for the other player. So I thought: jiujitsu must be something like a manifold.

## a surface of many dimensions

A manifold is a kind of “surface”: you can think of it as a landscape or a terrain—with the caveat that a surface as we usually understand it has only two dimensions while a manifold can have many dimensions, representing the (N-1)-dimensional surface of an N-dimensional volume.

In jiujitsu if you are holding someone in side control, we say you have the “top-side” position, while if you the one are being held down beneath the other person, we say you are in the “bottom-side” position. In the manifold model the top-side player occupies a peak, while the bottom-side player is constrained in the basin that peak describes when seen from the other side of the terrain. Your peak is their valley.

![A colored three-dimensional surface with peaks and valleys, illustrating a terrain of possible jiujitsu configurations.](/images/jiujitsu-is-a-manifold/manifold.png)

This invertability of perspective means that jiujitsu is an *involutive manifold*. An *involution* is the process of flipping something into negative space, a kind of turning it inside out. Involution is reversible, like negating a negative number gives you a positive number. So the same occupied area within the whole available configuration space of every way two people can be tangled up together can be both a position of advantage or disadvantage, depending on whether you're viewing the terrain from above or from below.

At any time you and your opponent occupy a mere sub-region of the vast landscape of possible configurations, but the whole terrain may seem to be turned inside-out in an instant, as if the smaller region you currently inhabit passed *through* the manifold to the other side. This happens whenever you swap sides of the same position, like when a turnover from bottom side control places you on top of that same side control position. You stayed in the same region—you just changed which side of the landscape you were on by swapping sides with your opponent.

![The same three-dimensional terrain inverted, with the previous peaks becoming valleys and the previous valleys becoming peaks.](/images/jiujitsu-is-a-manifold/inverted-manifold.png)

When you are occupying a peak—for example mount, top-side side control, or back mount, you can imagine an area stretching across that peak that's as large as your available space of decisions. There's a lot you can do from the top of a mountain that still keeps you on it. Your opponent, on the other hand, is on the other side of the terrain from you—in the underworld, the upside-down, and likely having a much worse time than you. And because on that side they occupy a *basin*—some kind of pit they have to climb or dig their way out of—their range of available motion and decisions is accordingly constrained. Their space of available decisions is constrained by the walls of that pit.

The goal of decisive victory via submission in jiujitsu is analogous to reducing your opponent’s space of available decisions until they are left with only one: giving up. You may do this by gradually reducing the volume they inhabit on their side of the terrain, steepening the walls they are forced to climb, until you compress the area surrounding them and all they can still do to a *single point*.

At that point, your victory and their defeat become one.

## involution

> *Once upon a time, in a temple nestled in the misty end of south hill, lived a pair of monks. One old and one young.*
>
> *“What are the differences between Heaven and Hell?” the young monk asked the learned master one day.*
>
> *“There are no material differences,” replied the old monk peacefully.*
>
> *“None at all?” asked the confused young monk.*
>
> *“Yes. Both Heaven and Hell look the same. They all have a dining hall with a big hot pot in the centre in which some delicious noodles are boiled, giving off an appetising scent,” said our old priest. “The size of the pan and the number of people sitting around the pot are the same in these two places.”*
>
> *“But oddly, each diner is given a pair of meter-long chopsticks and must use them to eat the noodles. And to eat the noodles, one must hold the chopsticks properly at their ends, no cheating is allowed,” the Zen master went on to describe to our young monk.*
>
> *“In the case of Hell, people are always starved because no matter how hard they try, they fail to get the noodles into their mouths,” said the old priest.*
>
> *“But isn't that the same thing that happens to the people in Heaven?” the junior questioned.*
>
> *“No. They can eat because they each feed the person sitting opposite them at the table. You see, that is the difference between Heaven and Hell,” explained the old monk.*
